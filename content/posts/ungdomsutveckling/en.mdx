# Youth development in alpine skiing: a long-term approach

A strong result in a junior race is one moment in a much longer journey. Young skiers develop at different rates, and an effective environment supports skills, motivation and continued enjoyment alongside competition.

This guide introduces long-term athlete development, the role of individual readiness and how parents and coaches can use feedback constructively.

## Children of the same age can need different support

Chronological age does not describe every aspect of development. Children in the same age group can differ in size, strength, coordination, experience and confidence.

Avoid treating current physical advantages or race results as a complete measure of long-term potential. Ask what each skier is learning, which tasks they can manage and how the training environment supports them.

## What is long-term athlete development?

Long-term athlete development, often abbreviated LTAD, is a way to plan development across stages rather than optimise only the next race. Alpine Canada's development pathway provides one example of this approach.

Use a framework to organise conversations with qualified coaches. It is not a guarantee against injury or burnout, and stage labels should not be treated as rigid deadlines for every child.

### Build a broad movement foundation

Early training can offer varied movement experiences, play and opportunities to develop basic skiing skills. Ask whether the child feels included, enjoys participating and has space to explore.

### Learn how to practise

As skills develop, help the skier understand a task, try it and describe what happened. Balance, coordination, rhythm and a clear technical focus can be discussed in language the child understands.

### Build training habits gradually

More structured training requires planning around the skier's readiness, recovery and other commitments. Coaches can help choose the appropriate progression in physical preparation and skiing tasks.

### Learn to compete

Preparation can gradually include tactics, course inspection and the management of competition routines. Review decisions and learning as well as results.

### Support performance and continued participation

Some athletes pursue elite racing; others continue skiing for enjoyment. A long-term approach should leave room to discuss motivation and future goals, rather than assume every child must follow the same destination.

## Physical preparation and recovery

Discuss strength, coordination and recovery with coaches who understand the skier's circumstances. Seek appropriate professional advice for pain, injury or rehabilitation questions. An online guide cannot assess individual injury risk or prescribe a suitable programme for every young skier.

## Motivation is part of development

Ask the child what they enjoy and what they want to improve. Make feedback concrete and manageable. Avoid turning every run into an examination or comparing children solely by race results.

Parents and coaches can agree who gives technical instructions so that the child does not receive competing priorities from several adults.

## Use video as a shared conversation

Choose footage that clearly shows the skier and skis. Let the child explain what they were trying to do, then discuss one observation. Compare similar tasks under similar conditions when following progress.
