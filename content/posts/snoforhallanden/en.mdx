# Snow conditions and technique: adapt to the surface

The ability to adapt to the surface is a useful skiing skill. Snow changes with temperature, moisture, terrain and the passage of other skiers. A technique that feels comfortable on one slope may need adjustment on another.

This guide explains how different surfaces influence grip and line choice, how race courses change and how video can support a discussion about adapting your skiing.

## Snow is not a neutral surface

Snow conditions influence the interaction between edging, loading and turn radius. Start by choosing terrain and speed appropriate to your ability and the available grip. There is no universally optimal edge angle for all skiers or snow conditions.

## Hard-packed or icy pistes

Hard snow can make changes in grip particularly noticeable. A controlled, progressive turn and appropriate speed matter more than trying to force a large edge angle.

- Choose a slope on which you can control your speed.
- Notice when the skis lose grip and discuss the pattern with an instructor.
- Consider equipment condition as well as technique.
- Avoid copying elite racing angles as a target for recreational skiing.

## Packed powder

A more forgiving surface can make some movements feel easier. If a skier is comfortable on packed powder but struggles on harder snow, compare similar turns before assuming a single technical cause.

Ask what changes: grip, speed, turn shape, confidence or the skier's response to the terrain. More than one factor can contribute.

## Granular and spring snow

Heavier, wetter snow can change resistance and the effort required to turn. Conditions may vary substantially between sections of the same piste.

Keep the task manageable and allow room to adjust. Discuss turn shape and timing with your instructor rather than automatically trying to use more force. If the surface is unfamiliar, start on easier terrain.

## Crust

A firm layer over softer snow can make the skis' response less predictable. Avoid treating a short online guide as sufficient preparation for difficult or unfamiliar terrain. Choose an appropriate environment and seek instruction when needed.

The useful question is how to maintain control in the actual conditions, rather than apply one fixed movement to every type of snow.

## Rutted surfaces

Ruts are a common feature of race courses. The line available in a rut can differ from the line on a smooth course. Discuss how to manage uneven terrain with your coach, including the relationship between the upper body and movement in the legs.

Watch several turns to see whether contact with a rut repeatedly disrupts the next turn. A video can show a pattern without directly measuring the pressure on either ski.

## How courses change during a race

Repeated runs can form deeper ruts and expose or polish harder snow. These changes may affect the available line and the room for adjustment. A later start does not automatically require one particular tactic; assess the actual course and your ability with your coach.

## Practise adapting your technique

### 1. Use varied, appropriate conditions

With guidance, practise on different surfaces within your ability. Change one task at a time and notice how the skis respond.

### 2. Compare video under known conditions

Note the snow, terrain and intended task when filming. Compare footage from similar situations when assessing progress. Different conditions can explain why two clips look different.

### 3. Turn observations into questions

If you lose grip on hard snow or lose balance in ruts, ask your coach to help identify the relevant movement and an appropriate exercise. Avoid diagnosing the cause solely from the surface type.

## Feedback that considers the context

A video can provide clues about visible snow conditions and movement patterns, but it cannot fully measure the surface or forces beneath the skis. Read Alpine Mastery's feedback alongside the clip, your own experience and your coach's assessment.
