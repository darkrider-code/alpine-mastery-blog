# Fatigue on the slopes: recognise changes in your skiing

It is your fifth slalom run of the day, and turns that felt straightforward earlier now feel rushed. Fatigue is one possible explanation, but snow, visibility, terrain and the task may also have changed.

## Fatigue develops during the day

Physical effort, concentration and recovery all matter when planning a ski day. Do not assume everyone passes through the same fixed sequence of fatigue stages. Start from how you feel and whether you can still ski the task with control.

## What can you look for in video?

Compare early and later runs on similar terrain. Look for changes in rhythm, balance, turn shape and timing. Note what the task was and how you felt when recording each clip.

A visible change is an observation, not proof that fatigue caused it. An ordinary phone video does not measure muscle fatigue or diagnose an injury.

## Plan for useful practice

- Take breaks before control and concentration deteriorate.
- Adjust terrain, speed and task when the day becomes more demanding.
- Discuss session length and recovery with your coach.
- Stop and reassess if you cannot complete the task with control.
- Treat pain or a suspected injury as a reason to seek appropriate assessment, rather than a challenge to ski through.

Physical preparation and recovery should suit your level and circumstances. Practising while exhausted is not a universal recommendation.

## Consider the whole season

Travel, training, competition and daily commitments can accumulate. Record how sessions feel alongside the training plan, and discuss recurring difficulties with your coach. A short video can support that conversation, but does not replace it.
