As the season approaches, parents' and coaches' phones quickly fill with clips from snow camps. But in sports science and motor-learning theory, a video's value is not the film itself, but *how* and *when* feedback is delivered.

For young competitive alpine skiers, the gap between proprioception (what the body *feels* it is doing) and actual biomechanics is often large. Without the right feedback, faulty movement patterns can be reinforced in muscle memory run after run.

### What research says about feedback and motor learning

Sports psychology and motor-control research commonly distinguishes between *Knowledge of Results* and *Knowledge of Performance*. In complex, high-speed sports such as alpine skiing, several principles stand out:

1. **The cost of delay:** The longer the gap between a run and feedback, the harder it is for the nervous system to connect the sensation of the turn with the correction. Watching footage hours later at the hotel loses much of the immediate learning effect on snow.
2. **Cognitive overload:** Showing a sequence without clear guidance often makes young skiers focus on the wrong details, such as snow spray or the surroundings. Feedback must be highly specific and stripped back to be effective.
3. **Actionable drills:** Simply pointing out an error, such as “you are leaning onto the inside ski”, creates uncertainty. Learning starts when the skier receives a direct countermeasure in the form of a specific drill.
