# Choosing your line: tactics and course inspection

At racing speed, decisions about the next gate need to be made early. Line choice is an important part of alpine skiing: strong technique alone does not guarantee a fast run if the chosen route leads to late turns and unnecessary braking.

This article covers how line choice relates to speed, the trade-offs between a direct and a more conservative route, course inspection, section analysis and using video to understand turn initiation.

## Line choice and technique work together

Course setting and slope angle influence the opportunities available to a racer. The skier still has to choose a route they can execute. Rather than treating line choice as separate from technique, ask whether the chosen line fits your current ability and the conditions.

When reviewing a run, look beyond the moment of gate passage. A late exit can affect the approach to several subsequent gates. The shortest route is not necessarily the fastest route you can ski consistently.

## A direct line versus a more conservative line

### A direct line

A direct line can involve earlier turn preparation, a shorter overall path and a precise exit. It can be effective when the skier has the skill and space to execute it. Small mistakes can also leave very little room for recovery.

### A more conservative line

A wider approach may offer more time and room to organise the turn and control speed. It can also increase the distance travelled or the time spent braking. The appropriate choice depends on the section, snow and skier; there is no single line that is best everywhere.

Use your coach's terminology when discussing turn phases, including the initiation and the steering phase out of the turn, sometimes labelled INI and STEOUT. These labels help identify a phase, rather than prescribe a universal duration.

## Make course inspection an active process

Use inspection to build a plan for skiing the course, rather than simply memorising gate positions.

- Identify changes in rhythm and gate spacing.
- Note offsets, slope changes and terrain transitions.
- Identify sections where maintaining control will influence the following gates.
- Discuss alternative approaches with your coach.
- Choose a small number of clear cues you can recall at the start.

A useful question is: “What needs to happen before this gate so that I am ready for the next one?”

## Use section analysis to ask better questions

Gate passage alone does not explain where time is gained or lost. If reliable timing data is available, compare sections alongside video. Consider whether a change in approach affected speed and control further down the course.

Without calibrated timing data, ordinary video cannot provide precise split times or prove which route is fastest. It can still help you identify where to ask the coach for more feedback.

## Ways to practise line choice

### 1. Free skiing with a planned turn shape

On appropriate, clear terrain, agree a turn shape with your coach and practise maintaining it. This lets you focus on the relationship between terrain, speed and turn preparation without concentrating only on the gates.

### 2. Video feedback

Watch when the new turn begins, how the skier approaches each gate and what happens after the turn's apex. Compare several turns and similar sections rather than one still image.

### 3. Explain the decision

Describe your intended route in your own words: where do you need more room, and where can you carry the turn into the next section? Compare the plan with what happened in the run.
