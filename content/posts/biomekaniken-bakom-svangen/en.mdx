# Carving on skis: understand the turn and review your technique

**Carving is a turn in which the skis follow their edges with very little sideways skidding.** How can you tell whether you are carving, and what should you look for when a turn does not work as intended?

This guide helps recreational skiers and parents review a skiing video: look at the transition, the shape of each turn and differences between left and right. Then choose one thing to work on.

*Updated 25 October 2026 with common movement patterns, filming guidance and questions about carving.*

## What is the difference between carving and a skidded turn?

In a carved turn, the skis mainly follow their edges through the snow. In a skidded turn, they also move sideways. Two clear, narrow tracks can indicate carving; wider, smeared tracks can indicate more skidding. Snow conditions and camera angle affect what you can see.

Skidding is not automatically a technical mistake. It is also a way to control speed and adapt your skiing to the terrain and other skiers. The goal is to choose an appropriate turn shape with control, rather than carve everywhere.

## Understand the three phases of a turn

Breaking a turn into phases is a practical way to review a video. During skiing, the phases flow into one another.

### 1. Transition and initiation

Watch how the old turn ends and the new one begins. Do the skis change direction gradually, or is there a sudden twist? Watch several turns before drawing conclusions from a single frame.

### 2. The steering phase

Follow the path of the skis through the middle of the turn. Is the arc smooth? Can you see the whole skier and follow both the skis and the upper body? Edging, loading, speed, ski shape and snow conditions work together. A large edge angle is not a goal in itself.

### 3. Finishing and starting the next turn

Watch the skier exit the arc. Is there time to start the next turn, or does a sudden correction follow? Compare left and right turns on similar terrain and at a similar speed.

## Why do my skis skid when I try to carve?

A single moment rarely explains the cause. Start with three questions:

- **When does the skidding start?** At the transition, in the middle of the turn or only at the end?
- **Does it happen in both directions?** A recurring difference is more useful to discuss than one isolated turn.
- **Have the conditions changed?** Steeper terrain, harder snow and greater speed can affect both the turn and your choice of technique.

Show the same sequence to a ski instructor or coach. Ask for one clear focus appropriate to your level, rather than trying to change several things at once.

## Three movement patterns to investigate when your skis skid

A skiing video can help you formulate a question for your coach. The following are observations to investigate. They are not reliable diagnoses from a still image.

### 1. An A-frame: the skis are edged differently

An A-frame often describes the lower legs pointing in different directions so that the legs resemble an A. Watch several turns: does this pattern appear consistently in the same direction? Camera perspective can change its appearance. Ask an instructor to assess the movement before trying to correct it by pushing your knees sideways.

### 2. The upper body rotates into the turn

If the shoulders rotate abruptly as the skis change direction, discuss how the turn is initiated. Follow both the skis and the upper body through the entire transition. One photograph cannot show which movement came first, and some rotation may be part of the intended technique.

### 3. A late, abrupt finish

If the skis initially follow a smooth arc but then slow down through obvious skidding, investigate when the change begins. Turn shape, terrain and an attempt to control speed can all contribute. Video shows movement; it does not measure when or how strongly you load the skis.

Ask for a drill appropriate to your level and practise one focus at a time on a clear, manageable piste. Do not try to create larger edge angles by increasing speed.

## Film your skiing so that you can review it

Ask someone to stand in a safe place beside the piste and film you. Choose a quiet, visible section of the slope and ski within your ability.

1. Keep the whole skier and both skis in view through several consecutive turns.
2. Avoid zooming in so far that the feet or upper body disappear.
3. Save the original video and use slow playback when reviewing it.
4. Note what you were trying to do and what felt difficult.

## Checklist: review the video and choose your next step

Watch the clip at normal speed first. Then review the transition, steering phase and finish in slow motion.

- Follow the path of the skis: where do they carve and where do they move sideways?
- Compare several left and right turns under similar conditions.
- Choose one recurring observation, such as an abrupt twist at turn initiation.
- Ask for feedback on that observation and choose an appropriate drill.
- Film again after practising. Compare footage under similar conditions.

For parents, a concrete question is often a good start: “What were you trying to do in this turn?” Use the video to support a conversation with your child and their coach.

## What can AI video analysis show, and what can it not measure precisely?

A phone video can support feedback on visible movement patterns, turns and timing. It does not directly measure force against the snow or pressure distribution under the skis.

Perspective, distance, hidden body parts and image quality also affect angle estimates. Feedback from an ordinary phone video is not a precise laboratory measurement of biomechanics. AI feedback can be wrong; consider it alongside your own experience and a coach's assessment.

## What does research say about carving?

Komissarov's study, *Dynamics of carving runs in alpine skiing. I. The basic centrifugal pendulum*, uses a simplified mathematical model to examine balance and dynamics in carved turns. It provides a perspective on the mechanics, but does not validate Alpine Mastery's analysis or establish one optimal edge angle for recreational skiers.

## Common questions about carving and skiing videos

### How do I know whether I am carving or skidding?

Watch the path of the skis and, if visible, the tracks in the snow. Narrow tracks and little sideways movement suggest carved turns. Compare several turns: snow and camera perspective can hide skidding.

### What edge angle should I use for carving?

There is no angle that suits every skier and turn. Speed, turn radius, snow, equipment and skiing level all matter. Start with control and an appropriate turn shape rather than a number of degrees.

### Do I need a large edge angle to improve?

No. Focus on control and a turn suited to your level and the situation. Do not copy an elite racer's angles as a universal target.

### Can I use a video from a previous ski holiday?

Yes, if the skier and skis are clearly visible, it can support reflection. You need a new video to see how you ski now.

### What should I do after my first analysis?

Choose one focus from the feedback and discuss it with an instructor or coach if needed. Practise under controlled conditions, then film again. [Read about ski analysis and membership](/en/ai-skidanalys).
